What Jordan Holds Dear

Jordan often explains why the kitchen matters. “Every Sunday, all of us crowded in there. Grandma Lena cooked, Eli stole bites from the pot, and nobody was allowed to talk about homework. It was the one place where I never had to be anyone in particular.”

Jordan is a student in a nursing program. There is a goal Jordan has carried since a hospital stay at age nine: becoming a pediatric nurse.

There is that kitchen, in the house on Maple Street where Jordan grew up. There is a worn recipe book, Grandma Lena's, with butter stains on every page and notes in the margins in blue pen. There is Sam, Jordan's best friend since seventh grade, and Eli, Jordan's younger brother. There is the night the university volleyball team won the regional final, when Jordan served the last three points.

These are the things that matter most to Jordan. The next four parts follow Jordan through one year.

The Flood

In April, after four days of heavy rain, the river behind Maple Street rose faster than anyone expected. At two in the morning, an evacuation notice arrived on everyone's phones. Jordan had ten minutes. Jordan grabbed a coat, a phone charger, and Eli's inhaler. The recipe book stayed on its shelf in the kitchen.

When the water went down, the house was still standing, but the ground floor was ruined. The kitchen walls were stained to shoulder height, the cabinets had swollen shut, and the recipe book was a gray lump of pulp. Eli said, “Oh no.” “It's just a book,” Jordan answered. Then Jordan walked out to the car and cried for twenty minutes.

The family moved into Aunt Marisol's spare rooms, forty minutes away. Sam drove over that same weekend with a borrowed truck and two friends, and they carried out what could be saved. Neighbors left casseroles on a folding table in the street. The insurance paperwork was long and confusing, and Jordan stayed up late helping Jordan's parents fill it out.

One night, Jordan opened a blank notebook and began writing down every recipe of Grandma Lena's that could be remembered. There were only eleven. It was a start.

The Injury

By September, Jordan was back on the volleyball court, a little stiff but happy. In the first game of the fall season, Jordan jumped for a block, landed awkwardly, and heard a pop in the right knee. The diagnosis was a torn ligament. It meant surgery, six months of rehabilitation, and the end of the season.

It also meant something Jordan had not expected. The nursing program's clinical placement, scheduled for spring, required long hours of standing and lifting. Jordan's doctor said it would not be safe. The placement would have to wait a full year. The goal Jordan had worked toward for three years suddenly had its finish line moved.

“I did everything right,” Jordan told the physical therapist, Dr. Okafor, in the second week. Dr. Okafor nodded. “You did. It still happened. Let's talk about what you can do this week.”

Slowly, Jordan found a few things to hold onto. Teammates took turns visiting. The coach asked Jordan to keep game statistics from the bench, and Jordan found that watching the sport from the side showed things that were invisible from the court. Every week, Jordan could bend the knee a little further.

Distance

In January, two more changes arrived. Jordan's parents learned that rebuilding the house on Maple Street would cost far more than the insurance would pay. They decided to sell it. Jordan went back one last time. The kitchen was empty and smelled of damp plaster. Jordan stood in the doorway for a while, then closed the door and did not take a photo.

A few weeks later, Sam, who had finished the degree early, accepted a job on the other side of the country. They hugged at the airport and promised to call every Sunday. The first Sunday, the time difference got in the way. The second Sunday, they both forgot.

To help the family with expenses, Jordan decided to take a semester off from the university and work part-time at a hospital cafeteria. It was not the hospital Jordan had imagined. But on slow afternoons, Jordan noticed nurses coming in for coffee after long shifts, and began talking with them about their work.

Eli started calling more often. Jordan found that talking with Eli was easier than before.

One Year Later

A year after the flood, Jordan stands in the small kitchen of a rented apartment, stirring a pot of lentil soup. It is Grandma Lena's recipe, made from memory. The notebook now holds thirty-four recipes, collected from phone calls with aunts and cousins. Eli is on speaker, complaining that the soup has too much pepper. Sam appears on the laptop screen, laughing, and asking for a bite through the camera.

The knee still aches in cold weather. Jordan will return to the university next fall, and the clinical placement has been approved. Jordan has also joined a peer support group for nursing students, and has already been asked to speak to first-years about taking time off.

The recipe book, the kitchen, the season, and the plan were all lost during this year, and none of them could be replaced. Eli and Sam are still part of Jordan's life. The notebook is still filling up. After dinner, Jordan washes the pot, turns off the kitchen light, and checks the schedule for the week ahead.
